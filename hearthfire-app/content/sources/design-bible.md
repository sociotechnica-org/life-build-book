
HEARTHFIRE
The Design Bible
A Life-Skills RPG for the Journey to College

Master Design Document • v1.0 • March 2026

1. Philosophy & Framework


1.1 What Hearthfire Is
Hearthfire is a life-skills RPG designed for a teenager with ADHD preparing for college independence. It transforms the ~18 months before college into a structured progression experience, replacing compliance-based approaches with a game-like system that builds real skills through engagement, reflection, and co-design.
The core concept: daily maintenance tasks plus meaningful quests, with visible progress and real stakes, inside a framework the player helps build.
1.2 Why a Game Structure
Game design solves three specific problems:
Invisible rewards. The payoff for adulting is far away and abstract. A game structure makes progress visible — XP accumulates, levels unlock, you can see what you've built.
Willpower failure. With ADHD, willpower-dependent systems collapse. The game provides external scaffolding — the structure holds the routine so the brain doesn't have to.
Surveillance resistance. Compliance monitoring creates opposition. Self-reporting plus co-design means the player is tracking his own expedition, not being monitored.
1.3 The Coaching Framework
Hearthfire implements coaching principles, not parenting-as-management:

1.5 Motivation Architecture
Hearthfire navigates the intrinsic/extrinsic motivation tension deliberately:
The main reward is independence. Car access, autonomy, reduced oversight — things naturally connected to responsibility, not arbitrary treats.
Leveling requires reflection, not just completion. Quest XP comes from thinking about what happened, not just doing the thing.
The frame is preparation, not compliance. He's building gear for an expedition, not earning points for pleasing parents.
He's a co-designer. He understands the motivational systems, within limits. He helped name the quests, chose daily missions, and shapes how the system evolves.
1.6 The Co-Design Principle
The player is both the first user and a co-designer. He proposed quest names ("Taming the Iron Beast" for car maintenance, "Escorting the Hermit" for taking his brother somewhere), daily missions, and gear he wants to earn. His design ideas are primary source material that shaped the system. When something feels wrong or annoying, that's a design insight, not a failure.

2. System Architecture


2.1 The Dual-Loop Structure
Hearthfire runs on two interlocking loops:
Daily Loop (Base Camp Maintenance): Morning and evening check-ins on foundational habits. These are the "keeping the lights on" basics. The daily loop unlocks gaming computer access.
Weekly Loop (Quests + Progression): Larger skill-building challenges across eight domains, plus weekly calibration and check-in. The weekly loop drives level advancement and unlocks car access.

3. Daily Operations: Check-Ins


3.1 Check-In Philosophy
The check-in is a self-assessment tool, not a compliance report. Surveillance asks "did you do it? Prove it." Coaching asks "how did it go? What got in the way?"
Even if self-reports are inflated, that's data — the pattern becomes visible over time through natural consequences. The goal is to build the habit of noticing, which is the actual skill underneath all the tasks.
Principles: self-reported (not verified), partial credit exists, reflection built in, quick enough to actually do, pattern-oriented (value is in accumulation, not any single day).

5. Quest System


5.1 Quest Structure
Quests are real-world skill-building challenges across eight domains. Each quest has a description (what to do), a reflection prompt (what to think about), and a difficulty rating that determines base XP. Quests are where the deeper curriculum lives — they build the actual skills underneath the surface tasks.
Pace: 1–2 quests per week, with flexibility to repeat, skip, or substitute.
Co-design: The player should be designing his own quests by Levels 3–4.

5.3 Reflection Quality Guide
Quests earn XP not just for completion but for reflection quality:
Level 1 (Basic — minimum for XP): Answered the prompt with some specific detail. "It went fine" doesn't count.
Level 2 (Solid — standard XP): Specific examples, identified what worked AND what didn't, some "what I'd do differently."
Level 3 (Deep — bonus XP): Connected to patterns across experiences, identified underlying causes, generated a principle or system change, honest about struggles.

Example prompt: "What got in the way?"
Level 1: "I was tired."
Level 2: "I stayed up too late on my phone, so I was tired in the morning and hit snooze."
Level 3: "I stayed up late on my phone, which I do most nights when I'm avoiding something — in this case, I was anxious about the test. The tiredness wasn't the real problem; the anxiety management was. I need a different strategy for pre-test nights."
5.4 Quest Selection Guidelines
Balance across domains: Don't let the player only do quests in comfortable domains. Rotate through all 8 over time.
Match to readiness: If a Level 3 quest in one domain is too hard, drop to Level 2 in that domain. Progress isn't uniform.
Include recovery quests: The "something went wrong, now what?" quests are some of the most important.
Co-design new quests: These are starting points. The player should be designing quests by Level 3–4.
The full quest library is maintained as a separate companion document, organized by domain and level.

6.4 Weekly XP Budget Analysis
At full engagement (~2–3 hours/week), the system generates 200–350 XP weekly. At 70% engagement, approximately 150–250 XP. This produces steady progression through the level thresholds: a fully engaged player reaches Level 2 in about a week, Level 3 by week 4–5, Level 4 by week 8–9.
The system is deliberately tuned so that imperfect weeks don't stall advancement. Missing a day doesn't compound into falling behind — it just slows the pace slightly. This is critical for ADHD: the system can't punish inconsistency so harshly that a bad week creates a motivation spiral.

7. Momentum Tracking


7.1 Design Intent
Momentum replaces the streak system. Streaks are fragile — one missed day resets everything, creating exactly the kind of shame spiral the system is designed to avoid. Momentum is resilient: it builds gradually, decays slowly, and recovers when engagement returns.
7.2 How It Works
Momentum is a score from 0–100 that reflects recent engagement pattern. It's informational daily but pays out weekly:
Daily visible: The player can see their momentum score each day, providing awareness of engagement pattern.
Weekly payout: At Sunday check-in, momentum score converts to a weekly XP bonus.
Decay is gentle: Missing one day barely registers. Missing several days in a row produces noticeable but not catastrophic decline.
Recovery is real: A good day after a rough stretch immediately starts pushing momentum back up.
7.3 Momentum Calculation
Each day earns engagement points: morning check-in (3 pts), evening check-in (2 pts), quest work (2 pts). Maximum 7 points per day.
Momentum uses a weighted rolling average, with recent days weighted more heavily. The formula emphasizes the last 3–4 days most, meaning a player can recover from a rough week within about 4 days of re-engagement.

8. Foraging & Materials


8.1 Foraging Replaces the Wheel
The original reward system used a wheel spin (like a slot machine) to deliver small variable rewards after check-ins. It had a fundamental design conflict: the reward landed in a single moment with no residual value. You spin, you get $0.50 gas money, it evaporates.
Foraging replaces the wheel with a system where rewards accumulate toward goals. Instead of a disposable payout, each successful check-in triggers a forage that yields materials (for crafting gear) or provisions (small practical rewards). Materials persist in inventory, visibly advancing toward something the player wants.

Provisions keep some cash flowing (roughly $15–20/month at steady engagement) but the primary economy is materials and gear.

9. Gear & Crafting


9.1 How Crafting Works
Materials accumulate in a visible inventory. When the player has enough of the right materials, they can craft a piece of gear. Gear items are real-world things the player actually wants — room upgrades, car accessories, tech, wardrobe items. The catalog was seeded from the player's own co-design session requests.
Crafting is instant once materials are gathered. No additional cost, no delay — the accumulation is the challenge.

10.2 Responsibility Protocols by Level
Level 1: Recruit
Errand runs only — grocery, pharmacy, shuttling brother, drop-offs. Defined purpose and destination before leaving. Direct there-and-back. Text when leaving (destination + ETA), on arrival, and when heading home. Not: joyrides, picking up friends, open-ended trips.
Level 2: Scout
Everything from Level 1, plus one social trip per weekend to a pre-approved friend's house. Daytime only (home by dinner or specified time). Destination communicated in advance (not "out"). Check-in text on arrival. Not: multiple stops, picking up passengers, evening trips.
Level 3: Pathfinder
Everything from Level 2, plus multi-stop trips, two social trips per weekend, one pre-communicated passenger, and extended daytime (home by 9pm). General plan shared ("going to Jake's, might get food, home by 8"). Text if plans change significantly. Not: party transportation, multiple passengers, late night.
Level 4: Ranger
Everything from Level 3, plus evening use (10pm weekends, 9pm school nights), multiple passengers, weekday use for approved purposes. Communication, not permission. Responsible about passengers. Consistent about curfew. This is "normal teenager with reasonable parents" level.
Levels 5–7
Progressive expansion to near-full (Level 5: 11pm, weekday free, longer distance), adult-level (Level 6: flexible curfew, road trips with notice), and full access (Level 7: no activation requirement, adult family member norms).

11. Gaming Access


Gaming computer access is the daily reward loop. It unlocks each day based on morning check-in performance.
Morning Score
Gaming Access
0–4 points
No access today
5–8 points

The 5/8 threshold is deliberately lenient — one full miss and one partial still qualifies. This prevents the system from becoming punitive on imperfect days while maintaining a meaningful gate. The check-in must be completed, not just the items — if the player doesn't self-report, gaming doesn't unlock regardless of what actually happened.
Gaming access has no end-of-day time limit built into the system. The evening check-in's wind-down tracking is informational. Screen time self-regulation is a Level 5+ skill goal, not a Level 1 enforcement mechanism.

13.2 Format & Tools
Start with paper (daily check-ins, weekly quests, a character sheet) and evolve from there. The co-design session identified paper as the preferred starting format to avoid phone rabbit holes. Digital evolution happens when the player requests it or when the system complexity warrants it.
Weekly check-ins double as product design sprints — roughly 1.5 hours on the weekend calendar for system review and quest work.
13.3 The Card System
Daily missions and quests are presented as physical cards in the Hearthfire visual style (dark background, amber/gold typography, fire/forge iconography). Each card includes the mission name, a flavor description, an "Ignites" section (what this enables), a "Shields" section (what this prevents), and mission steps. The Dawn March card (morning launch mission) is the template for this format.
